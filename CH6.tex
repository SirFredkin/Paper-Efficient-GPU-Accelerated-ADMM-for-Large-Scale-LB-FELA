
\section{Conclusion}

This work develops a GPU-accelerated ADMM framework tailored to large-scale lower-bound finite element limit analysis. The method combines factorization reuse, active-cone based penalty reweighting, coarse-to-fine warm-starting, and GPU-accelerated sparse linear algebra within a unified solution strategy. Numerical experiments on representative 2D and 3D benchmarks show that the proposed solver can achieve substantial reductions in time-to-solution relative to commercial interior-point solvers while maintaining close agreement in the computed collapse multipliers. 

The numerical results also reveal a clear difference between the computational behaviour of two- and three-dimensional problems. In 2D, the low cost of individual ADMM iterations, together with warm-starting and active-cone identification, provides a favourable scaling trend as the discretization is refined. In 3D, these algorithmic advantages remain effective, but the dominant computational bottleneck increasingly shifts toward sparse direct factorization. The stronger coupling and greater fill-in of the three-dimensional systems lead to larger numerical factors, higher memory requirements, and more expensive triangular solves. This ultimately limits the scalability of the present direct-solve implementation and motivates the development of indirect or matrix-free linear solvers for substantially larger 3D problems.


Overall, the principal advantage of the proposed framework is that it exploits the structure of lower-bound FELA at several computational levels simultaneously. Repeated ADMM linear systems permit factorization reuse, local conic yield constraints enable efficient parallel projection and selective penalty reweighting, and successive mesh refinements provide a natural hierarchy for coarse-to-fine warm starts. Combined with GPU acceleration, these complementary mechanisms provide an efficient and extensible computational pathway for high-fidelity lower-bound limit analysis, while also identifying sparse linear algebra as the key remaining challenge for further scaling in three dimensions.
