
\section{Discussion}

A vanilla ADMM implementation with GPU acceleration can already be competitive with IPMs on certain large-scale SOCPs. This is because ADMM internally benefits from cheaper iterations since the coefficient matrix remains unchanged throughout the iteration process and therefore is only factorized once, after which the factorization is reused in every subsequent \texttt{solve()}.

\subsection{Effect of solver enhancements}


Beyond this baseline, our work introduces two mechanisms that substantially improve ADMM performance on the large sparse SOCPs arising from lower-bound FELA in particular:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Active-cone identification with adaptive updates.}
  Since only a subset of cones is typically active at optimality, we periodically detect potentially active conic constraints and update the relevant entries of $D(\rho)$, which tends to improve convergence by emphasizing the active part of the feasible set. 

  \item \textbf{Coarse-to-fine continuation via warm-starting.}
  We warm-start fine-mesh solves with coarse-mesh solutions, exploiting ADMM's sensitivity to initialization to reduce the iterations required to meet the same stopping criteria. This idea is not limited to computational mechanics and has the potential to extend to other large-scale optimization settings involving a family of closely related problems.
\end{enumerate}

Both approaches substantially reduced the iteration count required for ADMM to reach the same termination tolerances. Also, with the adaptive updates on the penalty matrix $D(\rho)$, the convergence speed of ADMM now relies less on the initial selection of $\rho$ since the penalty parameter is adjusted dynamically during the iterations. The results also indicate that the required iteration count remains approximately independent of the problem size.

Here, we discuss the specific improvement made by each individual upgrade to the overall solver performance in the 3D rigid platen problem. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:Ablation_table}, the use of the three-dimensional ellipsoidal projection leads to the best overall performance among the tested configurations. Compared with the baseline \texttt{AUG + WS + CI} solver, \texttt{AUG + Projection} reduces the number of ADMM iterations from 180 to 140 and decreases the total solve time from 61.99s to 41.30s, while producing a comparable collapse multiplier. This indicates that the projection-based treatment improves the convergence behaviour of the solver for this problem. Moreover, the projection operation is naturally amenable to GPU parallelization and introduces only a small overhead, accounting for approximately 3\% of the total runtime.

The condensed formulation, by contrast, incurred the highest computational cost. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:Ablation_table}, its analysis and factorization times were substantially greater than those of the augmented formulation. This is primarily due to the increased fill-in produced during the direct factorization of the condensed system. Consequently, the memory required to store the factors exceeded the available GPU memory, introducing additional GPU I/O overhead and further degrading the overall performance.

The effects of warm-starting and active cone identification can also be observed from the comparison between \texttt{AUG + WS}, \texttt{AUG + CI}, and \texttt{AUG + WS + CI}. Using active cone identification alone reduces the number of iterations compared with warm-starting alone, from 600 to 280. However, it results in a longer solve time because identifying the active cones changes the matrix entries and therefore requires repeated matrix updates and re-factorizations. Warm-starting alone avoids this additional factorization cost, but requires significantly more ADMM iterations. Combining warm-starting with active cone identification provides the best balance between iteration reduction and factorization overhead between these two enhancements, reducing the solve time to 61.99s.




\subsection{Scalability bottleneck of direct factorization in 3D}


The availability of high-performance GPUs makes it possible to solve 2D and 3D FELA problems with adaptive mesh refinement and higher-order elements, often obtaining accurate lower-bound solutions within a few minutes. The direct solve routine is computationally efficient as long as the factorization fits within GPU device memory and does not require out-of-core memory I/O operations. Nevertheless, the proposed GPU-based direct factorization approach still faces a fundamental scalability bottleneck: its dependence on GPU device memory. As the problem size increases, especially in 3D simulations, the KKT system grows rapidly and the memory required to store the sparse factors can become prohibitive. This may ultimately limit the largest problem size that can be solved on a given GPU.

This limitation was not observed even in the largest 2D benchmarks. However, it became significant for large-scale 3D discretizations, even when the total number of variables was similar. The main reason is that the cost of sparse direct factorization is governed not only by the dimension of the linear system, but also by the sparsity pattern and the amount of fill-in generated during factorization. In 3D finite element problems, the factorization process typically leads to much denser matrices and substantially more fill-in than in 2D problems. As a result, both the numerical factorization and the repeated triangular solves required in the ADMM iterations can become dominated by memory traffic and I/O operations.

This difference is particularly pronounced in FELA. In LB formulations, each stress point involves more stress components in 3D problems than in 2D, leading to larger local blocks and bandwidth in the assembled system. The sparsity pattern of the $A$ matrix in FELA is demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:A_sparsity}.

Here, the 3D finite element connectivity introduces stronger and more spatially extensive coupling between variables in the inter-element equilibrium part of the constraint matrix $A$. Apart from that, the extra dimension increases the number of equality constraints required per stress point from 2 to 3 in intra-element equilibrium, and the dimension of the SOC constraints increased from $3\times3$ to $6\times6$ in 3D, when using Drucker-Prager or von Mises yield criterion.

Consequently, even for problems with a similar number of variables, the resulting KKT systems may exhibit much denser factors after reordering and factorization. This explains why the direct GPU-based approach remains highly efficient for the 2D plane strain cases studied here, but becomes more challenging for large-scale 3D discretizations. 

\subsection{Potential of indirect and matrix-free ADMM solvers}


Nevertheless, a more scalable direction may be to avoid explicitly computing and storing the sparse factorization altogether, and instead adopt an indirect linear solution strategy within ADMM. This is especially relevant for three-dimensional FELA, where direct factorization of sparse matrices can quickly become unfavourable due to excessive fill-in~\cite{Shewchuk_CG}. As noted in the broader numerical linear algebra literature, iterative solvers are often preferable, and in many large-scale three-dimensional applications practically necessary, due to the memory and computational costs associated with sparse direct methods~\cite{Saad_book}. Several ADMM-based solvers already provide indirect linear-system solvers~\cite{SCS, OSQP, COSMO, GeNIOS}. Notably, SCS has reported speedups from its GPU-based indirect implementation on large-scale problems~\cite{SCS}, suggesting that the combination of ADMM and indirect solves can be practical and scalable for sufficiently large instances.


\subsection{Generality, limitations, and future extensions}

The proposed method achieves a speedup over the IPM on many large-scale problems arising in FELA, while maintaining accuracy comparable to MOSEK under a similar accuracy target. We note, however, that part of this speedup is achieved using a GPU-based implementation, whereas MOSEK is run on a multi-threaded CPU. 

While the proposed acceleration techniques are developed in the context of LB-FELA, they target generic bottlenecks of first-order methods for SOCPs. Therefore, they may also benefit other sparse SOCPs with PDE-like structures. A systematic evaluation on broader classes of problems is left for future work.



Apart from the advances made in the GPU-accelerated ADMM solver, this study also implemented a simple but effective adaptive mesh refinement strategy that relies only on the lower-bound solution. The proposed framework was able to obtain a tight and strict lower-bound solution within one minute in 2D examples. This improvement stems not only from the acceleration provided by the GPU solver, but also from the computational efficiency of the higher-order Bernstein formulation~\cite{Bernstein_FELA}.

Future work will also consider more advanced yield criteria, such as Mohr-Coulomb and Lade-Duncan. Incorporating these criteria would increase the practical value of the proposed methodology, as they are widely used in geotechnical and engineering applications. Within the ADMM framework, such criteria can be incorporated provided that the projection of a general stress state onto the admissible yield surface can be computed efficiently~\cite{daSilva_2020, 3DLD}. For example, although not necessarily in the context of FELA, Clausen and co-workers implemented the three-dimensional Mohr-Coulomb criterion using a return-mapping strategy, in which the stress state is transformed into principal stress space, projected onto the yield surface, and then transformed back to the original stress space~\cite{Clausen_2007}. Extending this type of projection strategy to the present FELA-ADMM framework is a promising direction for future research.
